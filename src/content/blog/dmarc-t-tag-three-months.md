In May, RFC 9989 replaced the old DMARC specification. One of the smaller
changes was that the `pct` tag became historic and got a replacement called
`t`. Small change, easy to miss, and a good test of how fast the email world
actually moves when a standard says so.

So in August I pointed my DNS survey at the Tranco top million and wrote down
the starting line. The idea was simple: measure the same domains every month,
and watch `t` climb.

Three months in, it has climbed in the wrong direction.

## The numbers

Same million domains, three snapshots:

| | Aug 10 | Sep 10 | Oct 10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Domains with a DMARC record | 464,476 | 468,157 | 471,395 |
| …publishing `pct` (historic) | 122,751 | 122,713 | 123,290 |
| …publishing `t` (the replacement) | **686** | **685** | **672** |

In August the ratio was 179 to 1. In October it's 183 to 1. More domains have
DMARC than before, more of them publish the tag that's been retired, and
slightly fewer publish the one that replaced it.

I want to be careful here: 686 to 672 is a tiny move, and nobody should build a
theory on fourteen domains. But the point of the measurement was to see the
substitution start, and after three months there is nothing to see. If anything,
the line is flat with a slight lean the wrong way.

## Who actually moved

The totals hide some traffic in both directions, so I looked at individual
changes between August and October, among the 461,014 domains that had a DMARC
record both times:

- **2,480** stopped publishing `pct`. **2,259** started. That's a lot of
  churn for a tag that's officially been retired.
- **25** started publishing `t`. **41** stopped.
- **12** made the exact move the RFC describes: dropped `pct` and added `t`.

Twelve. Out of more than 120,000 domains that publish `pct`.

Of the 686 domains that had `t` in August, 643 still had it in October. 41
kept their DMARC record but quietly dropped the tag, two of them going back to
`pct`, and two removed DMARC entirely. My guess is that some of those are
domains whose record is generated by a provider or a tool, and the tool changed
its template. But that's a guess, not a finding.

## What people put in `t`

`t` is meant to take one of two values. `t=y` means test mode: apply the policy
one step weaker. `t=n`, the default, means apply it as written. In August the
686 split like this:

- `t=n`: 579
- `t=y`: 106
- something else: 1 (the value `s`, which isn't a thing)

And 8 of them published `pct` and `t` together, which is a bit like wearing a
belt with your braces. It's harmless, receivers will cope, but it does suggest
not everyone who added `t` read why it exists.

## The part about `pct` that annoys me

`pct` was always a slightly odd tag. It tells receivers to apply your policy to
a percentage of failing mail. The catch is that mail which isn't selected
doesn't get a free pass. It drops to the next weaker policy. Receivers applied
values between 0 and 100 inconsistently enough that the new standard gave up on
it and kept only the two values that worked: "all of it" and "test mode".

Here's the funny bit. Of the 122,751 domains publishing `pct` in August,
**109,539 set it to 100**. That's 89%. And 100 is the default. Nine out of ten
`pct` tags on the internet are asking receivers to do exactly what they would
have done anyway.

So the tag being retired is, for most of the domains carrying it, already doing
nothing. Removing it changes nothing. Leaving it changes nothing either, which
is probably why nobody does either in any hurry. Records like these get written
once, copied from a guide or a provider's setup page, and never touched again.

That's the real story in these numbers, I think. Not that people are resisting
RFC 9989, but that almost nobody revisits a DMARC record once it works.

## Why measure something this boring

Because it can't be measured later. There's no archive of what every domain's
DMARC record said last month. If `t` gets to 20% by 2028, the interesting
question will be *how*: steadily, in a jump when some big provider changed a
default, or only once receivers started caring. Answering that needs a series,
and a series needs a first point and every point after it. A month I don't
collect is gone.

So I'll keep collecting. The next snapshot is on November 10.

## What I'm not claiming

- **This is one population.** The Tranco list ranks popular domains. It's not a
  census of the internet, and its tail isn't a random sample of the internet's
  tail.
- **Lookups that failed are left out, not guessed.** Around 12,000 domains a
  month timed out or got a SERVFAIL. Counting them as "no DMARC" would bias
  everything downward by exactly the domains with flaky DNS, which aren't a
  random subset.
- **I don't know why any single domain changed.** I can see that a record
  changed, not who changed it or why.

If you publish `pct=100`, you can delete it. Nothing will happen, and that's
the point. If you use a lower value to ramp up enforcement, there's no direct
replacement for that, and
[the guide to what changed in RFC 9989](/guides/dmarc-rfc-9989/) covers what to
do instead. And if you're curious about the scanner behind these
numbers, it identifies itself and explains how to opt out on the
[research page](/research/).
